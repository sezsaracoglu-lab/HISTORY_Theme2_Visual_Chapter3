Lesson 01 · Foundational concept

Prejudice & Intellectual Snobbery

whiteboard

Key points

  • Prejudice = to prejudge — judgements built on prior beliefs, not the thing itself.
  • Merely labelling something triggers prejudgement, whatever the label (Billig & Tajfel, 1973). So everyone prejudges.
  • What differs is the response: being aware of prejudice is the first step to reducing it.
  • Forms include race, gender and caste/class — but also the seldom-named intellectual snobbery.
  • In psychology this snobbery produced two myths: that pure science beats applied, and that women cannot be psychologists.
Memory hook

Everyone prejudges; only the aware can disarm it. Label → belief → prejudgement → behaviour.

Lesson 02 · Terminology

Pure · Basic · Fundamental vs Applied

whiteboard

Key points

  • Three words mark the contrast with applied: pure, basic, fundamental — each with a different flavour.
  • Pure implies noble and good — making 'applied' sound dirty and bad by opposition.
  • Basic implies something done first; fundamental implies dealing with more important issues.
  • The labels are not descriptive but attitudinal — they encode the speaker's prejudice.
  • Applied research is simply work aimed at solving a practical problem — not inherently lesser.
Memory hook

Pure = noble, Basic = first, Fundamental = important. Three flattering masks for the same snobbery.

Lesson 03 · Social history

Why 'Pure' Was Believed Better

whiteboard

Key points

  • Pre-industrial Europe split into a leisured gentry/aristocracy (did 'pure') and everyone else who worked ('applied').
  • The Indian caste system mirrors this: Brahmin priests/teachers on top, Dalit manual labour at the bottom.
  • Universities taught the classics; practical subjects sat in technical colleges and only slowly moved in (physiotherapy 1992, nursing 2000).
  • Wundt branded his new Leipzig psychology 'pure' so it would fit high-status university life.
  • Useful results came anyway — reaction-time research solved astronomers' timing errors (Williams, 1912).
Memory hook

The leisured class did 'pure'; the workers did 'applied'. Wundt wanted psychology in the gentleman's wing.

Lesson 04 · The American turn

William James & Applied Psychology in America

whiteboard

Key points

  • European status was hereditary, so 'pure' ranked high; American status came from practical success, so applied was respected.
  • Hence applied psychology begins in the USA — science was valued because it solved problems.
  • Wundt = father of psychology as an institution; William James = father of American (applied) psychology.
  • Harvard's first 'Psychology' course (1872) predated Wundt's Leipzig teaching (1879) — but wasn't yet scientific.
  • James himself struggled with depression and neurasthenia; his works include Principles of Psychology (1890).
Memory hook

Europe inherited status → loved 'pure'. America earned it → loved 'applied'. So applied psychology was born in the USA.

Lesson 05 · Competing paradigms

Structuralism vs Functionalism

whiteboard

Key points

  • James drew on Darwin (structures fit their use) and Peirce's pragmatism (behaviour must be adaptive).
  • Structuralism (Wundt, Titchener): study the mind's structure for its own sake — the 'brick-wall' view.
  • Functionalism (James, Dewey, Angell): the mind has a function — it controls behaviour; consciousness is a flowing 'stream'.
  • Both relied on introspection, but used it for different reasons.
  • Angell coined the term 'functionalism' in his 1906 APA presidential address.
Memory hook

Structuralism asks WHAT the mind is made of; functionalism asks WHAT the mind is for.

Lesson 06 · Educational psychology

James's Advice to Teachers

whiteboard

Key points

  • James split behaviour into native reactions (instinctive: curiosity, fear, imitation, ambition) and habits (acquired, automatic).
  • Good teaching builds habits from native reactions — using movement and novelty to hook attention.
  • Learning works by association: tie new ideas to many old ones, so he argued against cramming (echoed by problem-based learning).
  • Citing Ebbinghaus: forgetting is fast at first then slows; recognition survives even when recall fails — so exams may be poor assessment.
  • Crucially, James never tested his recommendations on children — they are deduction from theory, not evidence.
Memory hook

Native reaction → habit, linked to many old ideas. Wise advice — but untested, so not yet science.

Lesson 07 · The impact question

James & Hall — Was It Applied Psychology?

whiteboard

Key points

  • James and Hall influenced education heavily — but never tested their theories on actual pupils.
  • By modern criteria their work created no 'impact': impact must be based on research, not observation-led theory.
  • Stanley Hall (1846–1924) was the administrator: first US psychology lab (1883), founded the APA in 1892.
  • Hall's 1904 adolescence textbook and his call for sex-segregated schooling shaped (now largely rejected) policy.
  • James (liberal) and Hall (conservative) clashed — Hall founded the APA while James was out of town.
Memory hook

Influence ≠ impact. Impact needs tested research; James & Hall had only observation-led theory.

Lesson 08 · Forensic psychology

Münsterberg & the Birth of Applied Research

whiteboard

Key points

  • Hugo Münsterberg (1863–1916), a critic of Wundt, is the father of applied psychology — invited to Harvard by James in 1897.
  • He defined applied research by the researcher's intention to solve a practical problem: applied is to experimental as engineering is to physics.
  • His forensic experiments showed witnesses are unreliable, people are suggestible (children more so), and false confessions occur under pressure.
  • His early lie-detection work: emotionally charged words produce much longer association times.
  • His three rules endure: gather testimony fast, avoid leading questions, and accept that sincere memories can be false.
Memory hook

Applied = engineering to basic science's physics. Münsterberg proved memory lies — even when the witness is honest.

Lesson 09 · Clinical psychology

Witmer & the First Psychology Clinic

whiteboard

Key points

  • Lightner Witmer (1867–1956) trained under Wundt, then founded the world's first psychological clinic in 1896 and coined 'clinical psychology'.
  • He shifted the cause of mental illness from heredity to environment — a poor environment harms, a better one heals.
  • This echoed earlier moral treatment (Tuke's York Retreat, Dorothea Dix) but placed it on a scientific, measurement-based footing.
  • His clinics were run by psychologists, not physicians — the start of psychology's role in mental illness.
  • Early psychologists offered measurement, not yet treatment; note Witmer was not influenced by Freud.
Memory hook

Witmer: blame the environment, not the genes — and measure it. The first clinic, run by psychologists.

Lesson 10 · Industrial psychology

Scott — Advertising & Personnel Testing

whiteboard

Key points

  • Walter Dill Scott (1869–1955) earned a PhD with Wundt but rejected the pure-only rule, pioneering industrial psychology.
  • In advertising, he assumed consumers are not rational: direct suggestion ('Use Pears Soap'), emotional appeals, and return coupons.
  • Return coupons worked because a small action by the consumer primes a future purchase.
  • In personnel testing, he had employers rate staff, then kept the test items that separated good from bad workers.
  • Like James he theorised, but like Witmer he produced real, research-based assessment methods.
Memory hook

Scott's two engines of industrial psychology: persuade the irrational consumer, and test the would-be employee.

Lesson 11 · Psychological testing

Binet, Spearman & the g Factor

whiteboard

Key points

  • Alfred Binet (with Simon, 1905) built ~30 tests for the French commission on schooling — measuring many separate faculties, not one number.
  • Binet believed you could be high on one faculty and low on another; his tests couldn't capture 'the totality of an intelligence'.
  • About 20 years later Spearman used factor analysis on correlated scores to infer a single general intelligence (g).
  • Binet's faculties guided pupils to classes within a school; Spearman's g was used to sort children into different schools.
  • The UK tripartite system (1944, 11-plus) followed g — later condemned as discriminatory; comprehensives arrived in 1965.
Memory hook

Binet = many faculties (sort within a school). Spearman = one 'g' (sort between schools). Same tests, opposite uses.

Lesson 12 · Politics of research

Applied vs Pure — and How the UK Government Won

whiteboard

Key points

  • Pure research long outranked applied in Europe — satirised by Swift's Laputa (extracting sunbeams from cucumbers).
  • UK polytechnics (1964) taught applied subjects at lower status; in 1992 they became universities — and dropped their applied mission.
  • The government valued applied work through funding competitions: the RAE (from 1986) rewarded research quality via journal articles.
  • The REF (2014) added a requirement to show impact — beneficial effect on society — weighted even more heavily by 2022.
  • Because universities chase funding, applied research gained a status it never previously had in the UK.
Memory hook

RAE rewarded papers; REF rewarded impact. Tie money to usefulness and even snobs will value applied work.

Lesson 13 · What impact means

Research Impact & the Three Intentions

whiteboard

Key points

  • Impact (per the REF) = reach × significance — and it must be based on research, not just influence.
  • Research sits on a data-led ↔ theory-led continuum: data-led impact comes from applied fields; theory-led impact can come from anywhere.
  • Three intentions: pure (intrinsic knowledge), applied (solve a problem), and translational (the bridge turning theory into benefit).
  • Example: quantum theory → mobile phones, but only via translational research — lab findings must be confirmed in real life.
  • Lewin's dictum frames it: 'there is nothing so practical as a good theory' (and theory-led programmes tend to progress, per Lakatos).
Memory hook

Impact = reach × significance, on a research base. Pure → translational → applied is the bridge between them.

Lesson 14 · Prejudice in action

Discrimination Against Women in Psychology

whiteboard

Key points

  • Two US bodies diverged: the pure-science Experimentalists (structuralist) treated science as a masculine club; the applied APA was less discriminatory.
  • The 'woman problem' simply meant women wanting to be psychologists; experimentalists barred them until 1928 (Eleanor Gibson only in 1949).
  • The APA made Mary Calkins its first female president (1905) — though Harvard refused her a degree despite her being its strongest student.
  • Edwin Boring tied prejudice against women and Jews to 'pure' science — the real driver was intellectual snobbery, not ability.
  • Evidence: girls lead in maths and literacy, boys in spatial skills — but with huge overlap and big SES effects, so sex is no basis for selection.
Memory hook

The 'woman problem' was just women wanting in. The barrier was snobbery dressed as 'pure science', not evidence.

Lesson 15 · Snobbery today

Academic Snobbery & University League Tables

whiteboard

Key points

  • The pure-over-applied snobbery has faded, but a descendant survives: university league tables.
  • Rankings rest mostly on research output, and the public rarely knows what 'good' actually measures.
  • The teaching–research nexus (great researchers make great teachers) is not supported by evidence — sometimes the link is even negative.
  • So a high-ranking university does not guarantee a better education: your effort and opportunities matter more.
  • Wellbeing note: students can struggle anywhere — even James despaired. Give it time, and talk to someone.
Memory hook

Rankings measure research, not your education. It is you, not the league table, that decides what you learn.
